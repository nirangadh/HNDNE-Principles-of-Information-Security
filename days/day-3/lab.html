Fingerprint your results file and store a password safely

Day 3, afternoon lab

Day 3, afternoon session. Duration 3 hours. Adds items 5 and 6 to Taxila's security register.

This lab sheet takes you from fingerprinting your own results file to storing a password the safe way. You will see one changed mark change the whole fingerprint, and a salt keep a password out of a lookup table.

The idea behind today's lab

Recall

A hash is a fixed-length fingerprint that changes completely on any edit.

Recall

A salt makes identical passwords store differently.

Common mistake

"A lookup table works the password out from its digest." It only matches the digest against digests computed in advance; it cannot reverse one. In segment 3 the table finds a common password at once, and finds nothing for your salted digest, because nobody computed that one in advance.

Common mistake

"A salt must be kept secret." A salt need not be secret; it must be unique for each user. It is stored in plain sight, beside the digest. Its job is to make every stored value different, not to hide anything.

Quick check

You and your lab partner both write the example line COHNDNE999X000,50, you with printf and your partner with echo. The two lines look the same on screen. Why do your digests differ?

  1. echo adds an invisible line ending, so the two files differ by one byte (answer)
  2. printf and echo use different hash functions inside
  3. The two terminals use different versions of SHA-256

Why: The hash takes every byte, and one extra byte changes the whole digest: the avalanche effect. Blaming the hash function is tempting, but SHA-256 is the same everywhere; only the input differed.

Quick check

In segment 2 your salt goes into your evidence pack, beside your salted digest, where anyone can read it. Is that a weakness?

  1. Yes: a salt is a key and must be kept secret
  2. No: a salt only needs to be unique, and is stored beside the digest anyway (answer)
  3. Yes: anyone with the salt can reverse the digest

Why: A salt's job is to make each stored value different, so no table built in advance holds it. Treating the salt as a secret key is tempting, but the further secret, kept apart from the database, is a pepper.

Objective

You will show that one changed mark gives your results file a completely different fingerprint, so any change can be detected. You will also show that a salt keeps a common password out of a lookup table, and that a slow password hash makes every guess cost more. This is how Taxila keeps published results honest and its password store safe, even if the store is stolen.

Steps

Each step states what to do and what you should see. If you do not see it, stop and work out why before moving on.

Segment 1: Fingerprint your personal results fileBoth tiers

Your results file is one line: your index number, a comma, then a mark. The mark comes from your index number by this rule: your mark is 50 plus the last digit of your index number. The example index ends in 0, so its mark is 50, and its results line is COHNDNE999X000,50. The file holds exactly that line: no space, and no new line at the end.

  1. Try the example first. Work through steps 2 to 4 with the example, and check that you get these values. Then repeat them with your own index number. You should see the line COHNDNE999X000,50 has the digest 84a521d52030ac8d64a96969eae32e2c4d3444d080b2d10efe7dc6bb0f4b4e3f. The changed line COHNDNE999X000,51 has the digest b2d1fd0d3c53d46bbb5db46bc922600ad8229bf70cd4840dbce892f6a8e0ef8a.

  2. Write your results line. Take the last digit of your index number and add it to 50. Write your index number, a comma and your mark, with no space. You should see a line such as COHNDNE999X000,50, with a two-digit mark from 50 to 59.

  3. Fingerprint it. Take the SHA-256 digest of your results line. You should see 64 hexadecimal characters. Write them down: this is the fingerprint the Registrar would publish beside the file.

  4. Change the mark by one and fingerprint it again. Add one to your mark, so 50 becomes 51. Take the digest again and compare the two. You should see a second 64-character digest that differs from the first in almost every character. You changed one character of the file; the digest changed everywhere.

Floor path

In CyberChef, type your results line in the input box, with no space or new line after it. Add the operation SHA2 and set its Size to 256; the default is 512, which is wrong here. For step 4, change the mark in the input box and read the new digest. Then open the avalanche demo, type your two lines into its two boxes, and read how many characters of the two digests differ.

Full tier, in a terminal on your Kali virtual machine. Replace the example line with your own:

mkdir -p ~/lab3 && cd ~/lab3
printf '%s' COHNDNE999X000,50 > results.csv
sha256sum results.csv
sha256sum results.csv > results.csv.sha256
sha256sum -c results.csv.sha256
printf '%s' COHNDNE999X000,51 > results.csv
sha256sum results.csv
sha256sum -c results.csv.sha256

The second command prints your digest, two spaces and the file name. The fourth command keeps that digest in a file, as the Registrar would keep it. The fifth checks the file against it and prints results.csv: OK. After you change the mark, the same check prints results.csv: FAILED and a warning that one checksum did not match.

Use printf, not echo, to write the file. echo adds an invisible line ending, which changes the file and so the digest.

Segment 2: Salt the password storeBoth tiers

Every student in this lab uses the same password: password123. It is a common password, and a poor one. That is the point: the whole room is now a group of staff who all chose the same password. Never type a real password of your own into any tool in this lab.

  1. Hash the password with no salt. Take the SHA-256 digest of password123. You should see ef92b778bafe771e89245b89ecbc08a44a4e166c06659911881f383d4473e94f. Look at your neighbour's screen: they have exactly the same value. In an unsalted store, everyone with the same password has the same stored value.

  2. Find your two salts. Take the SHA-256 digest of your index number. You made exactly this value on Day 2, where it was your Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) key. Your first salt is its first 8 characters. Your second salt is its last 8 characters. You should see for the example, the digest is 5b02a8f821835e23aa2d08921e40ddb0e0e21220f3e7d4e0a979bc9e962bb99b, so the first salt is 5b02a8f8 and the second salt is 962bb99b.

  3. Hash the password with each salt. Write the password, then your first salt straight after it, with no space: for the example, password1235b02a8f8. Take its SHA-256 digest. Then do the same with your second salt. You should see for the example, a0b12530aefa1e72505b5ef0afb82d54e8fc432477b2ade85f09d1174e42b535 with the first salt and f7bbb48d2b7e71a3f0d6310b067b2e257c3a5528987183ef5d92bd7ab46c0e5b with the second. The same password now gives two different stored values, and your neighbour's values differ from yours.

Floor path

In CyberChef, use SHA2 with Size 256 for every step. For step 6, put your index number in the input box. To read a salt exactly, add Take bytes after SHA2: Start 0 and Length 8 gives the first salt, and Start 56 and Length 8 gives the second. Then remove Take bytes again. For step 7, type the password and the salt together in the input box, with nothing between them and nothing after them.

Full tier. Replace the example index and the example salt with your own:

printf '%s' password123 | sha256sum
printf '%s' COHNDNE999X000 | sha256sum | cut -c1-8
printf '%s' COHNDNE999X000 | sha256sum | cut -c57-64
printf '%s' password1235b02a8f8 | sha256sum
printf '%s' password123962bb99b | sha256sum

The second and third commands print your two salts. sha256sum reads from the pipe here, so it prints a dash where a file name would be.

Segment 3: See why salt and slow hashing protect passwordsBoth tiers

The provided lookup-table.txt is a small lookup table: about a hundred common passwords, each beside its SHA-256 digest, computed in advance. Real tables hold millions. You only search it; you never add to it.

  1. Look up the unsalted digest. Search the table for the unsalted digest from step 5. You should see found at once, on the line that ends password123. The table did not reverse the hash: it simply matched a digest that someone computed in advance.

  2. Look up your salted digests. Search the table for each of your two salted digests from step 7. You should see not found. Nobody computed password123 with your salt in advance, so no table holds it. To fill a table with salted values, someone would have to compute it again for every salt, which means for every user.

  3. Time a fast hash. Take the SHA-256 digest of your salted password from step 7 again, and note how long it took. You should see a few milliseconds or less. That speed is good for checking files, and bad for passwords: it lets a guesser try millions of passwords a second.

  4. Time a slow password hash. Run the password through the Password-Based Key Derivation Function 2 (PBKDF2), with your first salt and 600,000 iterations. You should see it takes visibly longer, often a few seconds in the browser. The output is 64 hexadecimal characters; for the example it is e6f049ab50546fe6d115431d29fb0db710debf27b76b9a6a5236e21525f9b416. One login waits a moment, but every guess now costs the same moment, so a million guesses take far longer than before.

Floor path

For steps 8 and 9, open the salt-shaker demo, go to its panel "Check a digest", and paste each digest in turn. It searches the same table as the provided file. For step 10, take SHA2 with Size 256 of your salted password in CyberChef. Then read the bake time in the bar under the output: a clock symbol and a number of milliseconds. For step 11, remove SHA2 and add Derive PBKDF2 key. It ignores the input box, so set each of its boxes. Set Passphrase to password123, with its dropdown at UTF8, Key size to 256 and Iterations to 600000. Set Hashing function to SHA256, and Salt to your first salt, with its dropdown at UTF8. Type the iterations last, wait for the output, and read the bake time again. If you leave Salt empty, CyberChef picks a random salt and your output will not match.

Full tier. Save lookup-table.txt in ~/lab3 first: download it in the virtual machine's browser. Then run these commands, with your own digests and salt:

cd ~/lab3
grep ef92b778bafe771e89245b89ecbc08a44a4e166c06659911881f383d4473e94f lookup-table.txt
grep a0b12530aefa1e72505b5ef0afb82d54e8fc432477b2ade85f09d1174e42b535 lookup-table.txt
printf '%s' password1235b02a8f8 > salted.txt
time openssl dgst -sha256 salted.txt
time openssl kdf -keylen 32 -kdfopt digest:SHA256 -kdfopt pass:password123 -kdfopt salt:5b02a8f8 -kdfopt iter:600000 PBKDF2

The first grep prints the matching line; the second prints nothing, because the table does not hold your salted digest. time prints how long each command took: read the line that starts real. Expect a few thousandths of a second for openssl dgst, and many times more for openssl kdf. OpenSSL prints the PBKDF2 output in capitals with a colon between each pair, E6:F0:49:AB:... for the example; it is the same value as the browser's.

Segment 4: Prove it, break it, reset and write upBoth tiers

  1. Collect your evidence. Fill in the evidence pack table with your values from steps 2 to 9. You should see every value except the unsalted digest comes from your own index number.

  2. Draw and explain. Draw the salted-storage sketch and write your explanation, as listed under Evidence required. You should see a labelled sketch and at most 120 words.

PROVE IT Prove it

Your results file's two digests, before and after one change, differ in almost every character, and your salted and unsalted values for the same password differ. Both match the values your lecturer's recompute tool gives, because they come from your index number. The table finds the unsalted digest and not your salted one. Copy your results line, both digests, the unsalted digest, your two salts, your two salted digests and the two lookup results into your evidence pack.

×BREAK IT Break it

Fault: a single trailing space in the results file.

  1. Add one space at the end of your results line, after the mark, and take its digest. Symptom: a third digest, completely different from the first, although the line looks the same on the screen. For the example, COHNDNE999X000,50 with a space after it gives 17a67f960a43bb47094bf7a5aaeb019efa38f49d4d57846ee193600f0c3ec256.
  2. Evidence: the mark and the index are unchanged, so the cause must be a character you cannot see. A space is a byte like any other. The hash function takes every byte, and one changed byte changes the whole digest: the avalanche effect. On the full tier, sha256sum -c reports FAILED, and wc -c results.csv counts one more byte than before.
  3. Fix: remove the space and take the digest again. It matches your first digest. Write down the symptom and why it happens.

Full tier, to make and then remove the fault:

printf '%s ' COHNDNE999X000,50 > results.csv
sha256sum results.csv
wc -c results.csv
printf '%s' COHNDNE999X000,50 > results.csv
sha256sum -c results.csv.sha256

Taxila connection

This lab answers two of Taxila's requirements. Item 6 on the security register is results file integrity: published results that cannot be altered unnoticed, because any change, even one mark, gives a different fingerprint. Item 5 is the SIS (Student Information System) password store: passwords that stay safe even if the database is stolen, because the store holds salted, slow digests rather than passwords. The Registrar will ask whether a changed grade can now be noticed: yes, if the published digest is kept where the changer cannot reach it. The Head of IT (information technology) will ask what a thief would read in a stolen password store: digests and salts, but no password, and no table that matches them.

The idea behind today's lab

Recall

Confidentiality, integrity and availability are the first three properties.

Recall

Risk combines how likely a harm is and how much it would cost.

Common mistake

"Classification is about who owns the data." Classification is about how much harm a leak would cause. The Registrar owns both the course catalogue and the exam papers, but they sit in different classes.

Common mistake

"A risk row needs a tool." A risk row needs an asset, a threat, a weakness and the property at stake. You write all of today's rows with your own thinking and a spreadsheet.

Quick check

A lecturer suggests classing grades as internal, to save time. What would that allow?

  1. Nothing would change, because grades stay in the student records
  2. Grades could be shown to every student and member of staff (answer)
  3. Grades would then need more protection than exam papers

Why: Internal data is harmless if it leaks, so it may be shared inside the college. Grades harm one student each if they leak, so they are confidential. Where data is stored does not set its class; the harm does.

Quick check

Which of these is a complete risk row?

  1. Fee balances; a staff member who misuses access; every staff account can read all balances; 2 x 4 = 8; confidentiality (answer)
  2. Fee balances; every staff account can read all balances; likelihood 2, impact 4
  3. Fee balances; a careless lecturer as the vulnerability; score 8; authentication

Why: The first names an asset, a threat, the weakness it uses, scores that multiply correctly, and one property. The second has no threat. The third names a person as the vulnerability and a process, not a property.

Objective

You will sort every kind of data on Taxila's worksheet into one of four classes, and say where each important kind is most exposed. You will then write ten risk rows for the college's information technology (IT) office to act on. This answers the Data Protection Officer's first question and starts the college's list of risks.

Steps

Each step states what to do and what you should see. If you do not see it, stop and work out why before moving on.

Segment 1: Classify Taxila's dataFloor tier only

  1. Open the worksheet. Open data-inventory.csv. You should see seventeen rows, numbered 1 to 17. Each row names a system and one kind of data it holds. The other columns are empty.

  2. Name the structure. For each row, write structured, semi-structured or unstructured. Ask: does every value sit in a fixed column, does it carry labels but no fixed table, or is it free text? You should see records in the Student Information System (SIS) are structured; mailboxes are unstructured.

  3. Give each row a class. Write public, internal, confidential or restricted. Use one test for every row: how much harm would a leak cause? Check your answer against the classification page in the notes. You should see exam papers and national identity card numbers come out restricted; the course catalogue comes out public. Every class is used at least once.

Floor path

The whole lab is floor tier. Use the classification page in the day's notes, and the data-vs-information demo if you want to see again how a label changes what a value means.

Segment 2: Map the lifecycle and the property at stakeFloor tier only

  1. Find where grades are most exposed. Take grades (row 2). Walk it through the six stages: create, store, process, share, archive, destroy. Write the stage where it is most exposed, and the property most at stake. You should see the share and store stages stand out, and integrity is the property most at stake for grades: a changed grade is the harm Taxila has already suffered.

  2. Do the same for identity numbers. Take national identity card numbers (row 4). You should see store and share stand out again, but this time confidentiality is most at stake: the harm is someone reading them.

  3. Finish the restricted and confidential rows. Fill the stage and property columns for every row you classed restricted or confidential. Add one sentence in the last column saying why. You should see confidentiality is the most common answer, but not the only one. Grades point to integrity, and coursework submissions that must be there on a deadline night can point to availability.

Segment 3: Write the first risk-register rowsFloor tier only

  1. Copy the template. Open your copy of risk-register.csv. Read the example row first. You should see an asset (exam papers), a threat (a curious student), a vulnerability (a folder anyone can open), likelihood 3, impact 5, score 15, confidentiality, and a first treatment idea.

  2. Write ten rows. Fill R01 to R10. Each row names one real Taxila asset from your worksheet, one threat and one vulnerability. Give a likelihood from 1 (low) to 5 (high) and an impact from 1 to 5, then the score (likelihood x impact). Finish with the one property most at stake and a first treatment idea. You should see ten rows, each naming a real Taxila asset and one property, each score between 1 and 25.

  3. Check each row has both halves. For every row, cover the threat cell and ask: is the row still a risk? Then cover the vulnerability cell. You should see each row needs both. If a row has a weakness but no one who could use it, it is not a risk yet; rewrite it or replace it.

Segment 4: Prove it, check it, and write upFloor tier only

  1. Compare with a partner. Swap worksheets with the student beside you. Find one row where you chose a different class. You should see you can each give the harm your class is based on; agree which harm is larger.

  2. Sort your risks. Sort your ten rows by score, highest first. You should see the top rows are the ones the Head of IT should look at first. At least one top row touches an incident from last semester.

PROVE IT Prove it

Your worksheet places the exam papers (row 10) and the national identity card numbers (row 4) in restricted. Beside each, one sentence says why, in terms of harm: what would happen if it leaked.

×BREAK IT Break it

Fault: grades classed as internal.

  1. Change the class of grades (row 2) to internal. Now follow what internal allows: internal data is harmless if it leaks, so a lecturer could put every student's grade on a shared notice page. Symptom: every student's grade is readable by everyone.
  2. Evidence: the classification page says confidential data is personal data whose leak harms a person. Grades belong to one student each.
  3. Fix: change the class back to confidential. Write which property that protects (confidentiality: only the student and the staff who need the grade may read it).

RESET Reset

Taxila connection

This lab answers the first two requirements in Taxila's story. Knowing what data the college holds and how sensitive each kind is adds item 1, the data inventory and classification, to Taxila's security register. A living list of risks, each with an owner, adds item 2, the risk register. The Data Protection Officer will ask whose personal data sits in each restricted and confidential row. The Head of IT will ask which three rows to fix first, since the team is only four people. On Day 9 you return to these rows and choose how to treat each one.

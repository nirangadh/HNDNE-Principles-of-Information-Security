The idea behind today's lab

Recall

A key makes encryption reversible for the right person.

Recall

Symmetric encryption shares one key; asymmetric encryption uses a public key and a private key.

Common mistake

"The wrong key gives a partial answer." The wrong key fails outright or returns nonsense. Your message does not come back half right. Segment 3 shows you this with your own message.

Common mistake

"Encrypting to a public key means you can read it back." Only the matching private key can open it. In segment 4 you lock a message that you cannot open yourself.

Quick check

This lab makes your AES key from your index number, by a recipe printed on this page. Why would that be a poor key for real exam papers?

  1. AES needs a key made from a password instead
  2. A key must be kept as long as the message
  3. Anyone who knows your index and the recipe can make the same key (answer)

Why: Kerckhoffs's principle says the method may be public, so the key alone must be secret. Here the recipe is public and an index number is not secret, so the key is not secret either. It is fine today only because the message is not a secret.

Quick check

In segment 4, you encrypt your AES key to the module's public key. Who can open what you made?

  1. Only the holder of the module's private key (answer)
  2. You, because you made it
  3. Anyone who has the public key

Why: What a public key locks, only the matching private key opens. Making it yourself is tempting as a reason, but you hold only the public key, and a public key cannot open what it locked.

Objective

You will break a shift cipher by hand, then encrypt your own index number with AES so that only the key returns it. You will also lock a message with a public key. This is how Taxila's exam paper store keeps papers unreadable, even if the folder leaks.

Steps

Each step states what to do and what you should see. If you do not see it, stop and work out why before moving on.

Segment 1: Break a shift cipher by handBoth tiers

  1. Count the letters. Open frequency-bars and choose the lab's message, or paste the text of shift-cipher.txt into it. Find the tallest bar. In English the commonest letter is usually e. You should see one ciphertext letter stands far above the rest. If it stands for e, you can work out the shift: count how many places it sits after e in the alphabet.

  2. Check the shift with the wheel. Open caesar-wheel, choose the lab's message, and turn the wheel back by the shift you found. Then press the button that tries every key, to confirm. You should see a readable message from Taxila's examinations office. Write down the shift and the first sentence of the plaintext.

Floor path

Both tiers do segment 1 in the browser, with the two demos. You can also work on paper: write the alphabet twice, one row under the other, and slide the lower row by the shift.

Segment 2: Encrypt your personal message with the Advanced Encryption StandardBoth tiers

Your evidence must be your own, and your lecturer must be able to check it. So your key and your starting value are made from your index number by a fixed recipe. The recipe uses SHA-256 (Secure Hash Algorithm, 256 bits). Day 3 explains what SHA-256 is. Today it is only a recipe that turns your index number into a value of the right length.

Note

A real key is chosen at random and kept secret. This lab's key is made from your index number, by a recipe printed on this page. Anyone who knows your index could make it, so it would be a poor real key. It is fine here, because today's message is not a secret: the point is to see how the key works.

  1. Try the recipe on the example first. Work through steps 4 to 6 with the example COHNDNE999X000, and check that you get these values. Then repeat them with your own index number. You should see for the example, the key is 5b02a8f821835e23aa2d08921e40ddb0e0e21220f3e7d4e0a979bc9e962bb99b, the starting value is 0def77507f34a59d5dbf643c1c6e82b9, and the ciphertext is bc6b159611b059c4db6b8eb5b1ac2add.

  2. Make your key. Your plaintext is your index number, with nothing before or after it. Take the SHA-256 of your plaintext. You should see 64 hexadecimal characters. That is 32 bytes, which is 256 bits: an AES-256 key.

  3. Make your starting value. Write your index number backwards, by hand. Check it with CyberChef's Reverse operation. Take the SHA-256 of the reversed index, and keep only its first 32 hexadecimal characters. You should see 32 hexadecimal characters, which is 16 bytes. Cipher block chaining (CBC) mixes this starting value into the first block.

  4. Encrypt your plaintext. Encrypt your plaintext with AES-256 in CBC mode, using your key and your starting value. You should see 32 hexadecimal characters for a 14-character index number. You cannot read them. They are the same on both tiers, and the same as your lecturer's recompute tool gives.

Floor path

In CyberChef, type your index number in the input box, with no space or new line after it. For step 4, add SHA2 and set its Size to 256; the default is 512, which is wrong here. For step 5, put your reversed index in the input box, add SHA2 with Size 256, then Take bytes with Start 0 and Length 32. For step 6, put your index number back in the input box and use one operation only: AES Encrypt. Put your key in Key and your starting value in IV (initialisation vector), both set to HEX. Set Mode to CBC, Input to Raw and Output to Hex.

Full tier, in a terminal on your Kali virtual machine. Replace the example with your own index number, and the reversed example with your own reversed index:

mkdir -p ~/lab2 && cd ~/lab2
printf '%s' COHNDNE999X000 > plain.txt
printf '%s' COHNDNE999X000 | sha256sum | cut -c1-64
printf '%s' 000X999ENDNHOC | sha256sum | cut -c1-32
openssl enc -aes-256-cbc -nosalt -K <your key> -iv <your starting value> -in plain.txt -out cipher.bin
xxd -p cipher.bin

The third command prints your key and the fourth your starting value. Copy each one into the openssl command in place of <your key> and <your starting value>.

Use printf, not echo, to write the plaintext. echo adds an invisible line ending, which changes the plaintext and so the ciphertext. If your virtual machine says xxd: command not found, run sudo apt install xxd and try again.

Segment 3: The wrong keyBoth tiers

  1. Decrypt with your key. Decrypt your ciphertext with your key and your starting value. You should see exactly your index number, with nothing missing and nothing added.

  2. Decrypt with a changed key. Copy your key and change its last character to a different hexadecimal digit, for example b to c. Decrypt again with the changed key. You should see it fails. The full tier prints the words "bad decrypt" and an error line. CyberChef usually shows the error Unable to decrypt input with these parameters., and sometimes an empty or garbled result. You never see your index number, or part of it.

Floor path

In CyberChef, put your ciphertext in the input box and use AES Decrypt, with Key and IV set to HEX, Mode CBC, Input Hex and Output Raw.

Full tier:

openssl enc -d -aes-256-cbc -nosalt -K <your key> -iv <your starting value> -in cipher.bin
openssl enc -d -aes-256-cbc -nosalt -K <your changed key> -iv <your starting value> -in cipher.bin

The first command prints your index number. Your prompt appears on the same line, because your plaintext has no line ending.

Segment 4: Encrypt to a public key and meet the hybrid ideaBoth tiers

  1. Encrypt your plaintext to the module's public key. Use the provided module-public-key.txt with the Rivest, Shamir and Adleman method (RSA). You should see 256 bytes of ciphertext, which is 512 hexadecimal characters. Run it again and the result is different. That is normal: the method adds random padding each time.

  2. Try to open it yourself. You hold only the public key. You should see you cannot open it. CyberChef's RSA Decrypt asks for a private key, which you do not have. On the full tier, openssl refuses, saying a private key is needed.

  3. Carry your AES key the same way. Encrypt your AES key, the 64 characters from step 4, to the public key. You should see another 512 hexadecimal characters. This is the hybrid pattern done by hand. The slow public-key method carries only the short AES key, and the fast AES key carries the message. Your lecturer, who holds the private key, opens two volunteers' files in front of the class.

Floor path

In CyberChef, put your index number in the input box and use RSA Encrypt. Paste the whole text of module-public-key.txt into RSA Public Key (PEM), including its first and last lines. Keep Encryption Scheme RSA-OAEP and Message Digest Algorithm SHA-1. Then add To Hex with Delimiter None. For step 11, put your key in the input box instead.

Full tier. Save module-public-key.txt in ~/lab2 first: download it in the virtual machine's browser, or paste its text into nano module-public-key.txt.

openssl pkeyutl -encrypt -pubin -inkey module-public-key.txt -pkeyopt rsa_padding_mode:oaep -in plain.txt -out to-module.bin
wc -c to-module.bin
openssl pkeyutl -decrypt -pubin -inkey module-public-key.txt -in to-module.bin
printf '%s' <your key> > key.txt
openssl pkeyutl -encrypt -pubin -inkey module-public-key.txt -pkeyopt rsa_padding_mode:oaep -in key.txt -out key-to-module.bin

The second command shows 256 to-module.bin. The third fails, because a public key cannot open what it locked.

Segment 5: Prove it, break it, reset and write upBoth tiers

  1. Collect your evidence. Fill in the evidence pack table with your values from steps 4 to 8, and the shift and plaintext from segment 1. You should see every value comes from your own index number.

  2. Draw and explain. Draw the two-key sketch and write your explanation, as listed under Evidence required. You should see a labelled sketch and at most 120 words.

PROVE IT Prove it

Decrypting your ciphertext with your key returns exactly your index number, and the changed key does not. Your ciphertext equals the value your lecturer's recompute tool gives, because your key and your starting value come from your index number. Copy your plaintext, key, starting value, ciphertext, correct decryption and changed-key result into your evidence pack.

×BREAK IT Break it

Fault: one character of the ciphertext changed.

  1. Copy your ciphertext and change its last character to a different hexadecimal digit. Decrypt it with your correct key and starting value. Symptom: decryption fails, just as it did with the wrong key: "bad decrypt" on the full tier, and in CyberChef usually the error, sometimes an empty or garbled result.
  2. Evidence: the key and the starting value are unchanged, so the cause must be the ciphertext. AES works on blocks of 16 bytes. One changed character scrambles the whole block when it is decrypted. The end of every message carries a few filler bytes of a fixed pattern, called padding, and the decrypter checks them. Scrambled filler fails the check, so it reports an error.
  3. Fix: decrypt your original ciphertext again and see your index number return. Write down the symptom and why it happens.

Full tier, to make the changed file:

printf '%s' <your changed ciphertext> | xxd -r -p > broken.bin
openssl enc -d -aes-256-cbc -nosalt -K <your key> -iv <your starting value> -in broken.bin

RESET Reset

  1. Copy your evidence into your evidence pack first.
  2. Floor tier: clear CyberChef's recipe and input. Your module folder keeps the two provided files.
  3. Full tier: delete the lab folder with rm -r ~/lab2, then restore the day-2-start snapshot.
